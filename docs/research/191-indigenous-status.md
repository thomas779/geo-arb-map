# Indigenous nationhood in the USA and Canada, and why it is not a citizenship route

Research date: 2026-08-10. Companion to `191-origin-africa.json` and the wider origin-based
research family. **This memo exists so that nobody models tribal enrollment as a citizenship
route by mistake.** It produces no atlas rows on purpose.

Every quotation below was re-fetched from the URL given and matched character-for-character
after whitespace collapsing, using the same extraction the repository's quote gate uses. Where a
thing could not be verified it is flagged in bold and left unasserted.

---

## The answer in one paragraph

Tribal citizenship in a US federally recognised nation, Indian status under Canada's *Indian Act*,
Métis citizenship and Inuit enrolment under a land claims agreement are all membership of polities
that the international system does not treat as sovereign states. **None of them confers US or
Canadian citizenship, and none of them shortens the naturalisation clock.** US citizenship for
Indigenous people rests on *jus soli* — birth in the United States — plus the Indian Citizenship Act
1924, not on membership; Canadian citizenship is birth in Canada, descent, or a ministerial grant,
and the *Citizenship Act* does not contain the word "Indian" anywhere. What Indigenous membership
does buy is **mobility**, and that part is real: 8 U.S.C. § 1359 gives American Indians born in
Canada who possess at least 50 per centum of American Indian blood the right to pass the US border,
and 8 CFR 289.2 converts a qualifying entry into lawful permanent residence with no petition and no
fee. That is a green card handed over at the border. It is not citizenship, and the ordinary
five-year naturalisation residence requirement still runs from it.

## 1. United States — citizenship is territorial, not tribal

### 8 U.S.C. § 1401(b)

Verified at
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title8/html/USCODE-2023-title8-chap12-subchapIII-partI-sec1401.htm>

> The following shall be nationals and citizens of the United States at birth:

> (b) a person born in the United States to a member of an Indian, Eskimo, Aleutian, or other aboriginal tribe:

> That the granting of citizenship under this subsection shall not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of such person to tribal or other property;

Read the operative fact carefully: **born in the United States**. Tribal membership describes the
parent; it is not the source of the citizenship. The subsection exists because *Elk v. Wilkins*
(1884) held that a person born into a tribe was not born "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United
States for Fourteenth Amendment purposes, and Congress had to say otherwise. It does nothing at all
for a tribal member born outside the United States.

### The Indian Citizenship Act 1924

The Statutes at Large text is **not** on govinfo — that collection begins at volume 65 (1951). Two
official substitutes verify instead.

The codification and repeal chain, at
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title8/html/USCODE-2024-title8-chap1-sec1.htm>:

> Sections 3 to 3c, related to citizenship of Indians. Section 3 was from acts Feb. 8, 1887, ch. 119, §6, 24 Stat. 390; Mar. 3, 1901, ch. 868, 31 Stat. 1447; May 8, 1906, ch. 2348, 34 Stat. 182; Nov. 6, 1919, ch. 95, 41 Stat. 350; Mar. 3, 1921, ch. 120, §3, 41 Stat. 1250; June 2, 1924, ch. 233, 43 Stat. 253; Oct. 14, 1940, ch. 876, title I, subch. V, §504, 54 Stat. 1173.

And the operative words of the Act itself, quoted by the State Department at 8 FAM 301.1-7,
<https://fam.state.gov/FAM/08FAM/08FAM030101.html>:

> That all non-citizen Indians born within the territorial limits of the United States be, and they are hereby, declared to be citizens of the United States:

> Provided, That the granting of such citizenship shall not in any manner impair or otherwise affect the right of any Indian to tribal or other property.

The 1924 Act was a one-off collective declaration keyed to **birth within the territorial limits of
the United States**. It was spent on enactment and carried forward prospectively through Nationality
Act § 201(b) into what is now § 1401(b). The same FAM page states the through-line:

> Section 201(b) NA, effective January 13, 1941, declared that persons born in the United States to members of an Indian, Eskimo, Aleutian, or other aboriginal tribe were nationals and citizens of the United States at birth.

## 2. The Jay Treaty right — what it actually is

### 8 U.S.C. § 1359 (INA § 289), in full

Verified at
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title8/html/USCODE-2023-title8-chap12-subchapII-partIX-sec1359.htm>

Heading: `§1359. Application to American Indians born in Canada`

> Nothing in this subchapter shall be construed to affect the right of American Indians born in Canada to pass the borders of the United States, but such right shall extend only to persons who possess at least 50 per centum of blood of the American Indian race.

That single sentence is the entire section. Source credit: `(June 27, 1952, ch. 477, title II, ch. 9, §289, 66 Stat. 234.)`

Three things follow, and all three are routinely got wrong.

**It is a border-crossing right, not a nationality right.** The words are "pass the borders". Nothing
in § 1359 says anything about citizenship, naturalisation, or the right to a passport.

**The test is blood quantum, not enrolment.** A tribe's own citizenship decision is neither necessary
nor sufficient. 8 CFR 289.1, verified at
<https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-289>:

> The term American Indian born in Canada as used in section 289 of the Act includes only persons possessing 50 per centum or more of the blood of the American Indian race. It does not include a person who is the spouse or child of such an Indian or a person whose membership in an Indian tribe or family is created by adoption, unless such person possesses at least 50 per centum or more of such blood.

Note where the adoption exclusion lives: in the **regulation**, not in the statute. The statute is
the one sentence quoted above and contains no such carve-out.

**It converts into permanent residence.** 8 CFR 289.2, same URL:

> Any American Indian born in Canada who at the time of entry was entitled to the exemption provided for such person by the Act of April 2, 1928 (45 Stat. 401), or section 289 of the Act, and has maintained residence in the United States since his entry, shall be regarded as having been lawfully admitted for permanent residence.

USCIS states the practical effect, verified at
<https://www.uscis.gov/green-card/other-ways-get-green-card/green-card-american-indian-born-canada>:

> American Indians born in Canada (with at least 50% American Indian blood) cannot be denied admission to the United States. However, USCIS will create a record of admission for permanent residence if an American Indian born in Canada wishes to reside permanently in the United States.

> You cannot apply for permanent residence if your tribal membership is derived through marriage or adoption.

**This is the only sense in which Indigenous membership "accelerates" anything.** It removes the
immigrant-visa step — no petition, no priority date, no fee. It removes nothing from the
naturalisation requirements that follow. If the atlas ever wanted to record this it would be a
residence route, not a citizenship one, and it belongs to the United States, not to any tribal
nation.

## 3. Canada — Indian status is not, and does not lead to, citizenship

### How Canadian citizenship is acquired

*Citizenship Act*, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-29, s. 3(1), verified at
<https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-29/page-1.html>:

> 3 (1) Subject to this Act, a person is a citizen if (a) the person was born in Canada after February 14, 1977;

The remaining paragraphs are birth outside Canada to a citizen parent, grant under s. 5 or s. 11,
grant under s. 5.1 on adoption, and legacy/restoration cases. **Indian status is not among them.** A
full-text fetch of the Act at <https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-29/FullText.html> returns
zero occurrences of the word "Indian" anywhere in the statute.

The only discretionary lever is s. 5(4), verified at the same URL:

> (4) Despite any other provision of this Act, the Minister may, in his or her discretion, grant citizenship to any person to alleviate cases of statelessness or of special and unusual hardship or to reward services of an exceptional value to Canada.

That is a general hardship power. It is not an Indigenous channel.

### What Indian status *is*

*Indian Act*, R.S.C. 1985, c. I-5, s. 6, verified at
<https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-5/page-1.html>:

> 6 (1) Subject to section 7, a person is entitled to be registered if (a) that person was registered or entitled to be registered immediately before April 17, 1985;

> (2) Subject to section 7, a person is entitled to be registered if one of their parents is entitled to be registered under subsection (1) or, if that parent is no longer living, was so entitled at the time of death.

A pure descent test, administered by Canada, with **no residence, domicile or place-of-birth
requirement**. That last point matters for the symmetry question below. A full-text fetch of the
*Indian Act* finds no citizenship-conferring provision anywhere in it.

### What status does buy: a right of entry

*Immigration and Refugee Protection Act*, s. 19(1), verified at
<https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/I-2.5/FullText.html>. Marginal note: *Right of entry of
citizens and Indians*.

> 19 (1) Every Canadian citizen within the meaning of the Citizenship Act and every person registered as an Indian under the Indian Act has the right to enter and remain in Canada in accordance with this Act, and an officer shall allow the person to enter Canada if satisfied following an examination on their entry that the person is a citizen or registered Indian.

The same status recurs at IRPA s. 45(a):

> (a) recognize the right to enter Canada of a Canadian citizen within the meaning of the Citizenship Act , a person registered as an Indian under the Indian Act or a permanent resident;

(The stray space before the comma is an artefact of the italicised statute title in the source HTML,
and is reproduced here because the quote gate compares literally.)

## 4. Does the reverse Jay Treaty right hold?

No — but not for the reason usually given, and the usual framing needs correcting.

The Canadian right is keyed to **registration under the *Indian Act***: a legal-administrative
status, not Indigenous descent as such and not membership of a US tribe. A citizen of a US tribe with
no Canadian ancestry has no s. 19(1) right and is an ordinary foreign national at the Canadian
border.

But *Indian Act* s. 6, quoted above, imposes **no birthplace test**. A US-born person descended from
a registered parent can be entitled to registration, and once registered holds the full s. 19(1)
right to enter *and remain* — which is, in that narrow respect, more generous than § 1359, since it
carries no blood-quantum threshold and includes the right to remain rather than merely to cross.

So the asymmetry is not "US-born Indigenous people are excluded". It is that **Canada uses its own
statutory register, while the United States uses a blood-quantum test applied to any American Indian
born in Canada.** Two different keys to two different doors. Neither door is citizenship.

### Legislative state of play — a negative finding

The entire LEGISinfo bill database — 7,142 bills across every session on record, enumerated via
<https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bills/json> — contains **no** federal bill, Senate or Commons, to
implement Jay Treaty border rights for US-born Indigenous people. Bills S-235 (44-1) and C-274
(45-1), both titled *An Act to amend the Citizenship Act and the Immigration and Refugee Protection
Act*, concern young people ageing out of child welfare care and have nothing to do with Indigenous
mobility (<https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-274/first-reading>).

What is live and adjacent: **Bill S-2 (45-1), *An Act to amend the Indian Act (new registration
entitlements)***, at House committee. It expands who is entitled to registration under s. 6, and so
indirectly widens the class holding the IRPA s. 19(1) entry right. It is a status bill, not a border
or citizenship bill.

## 5. Haudenosaunee passports and other tribal travel documents

**No explicit official statement rejecting Haudenosaunee passports could be verified for this memo.**
state.gov's own search surfaces nothing for "Haudenosaunee"; the 2009-2017 press-briefing archive
returns `forbidden` on every transcript, so the July 2010 briefings around the Iroquois Nationals
lacrosse team could not be read; a govinfo Federal Register full-text search for `Haudenosaunee`
returns only NAGPRA repatriation notices, and `"tribal passport"` returns nothing at all. The
well-known 2010 lacrosse episode is therefore **news colour and is not sourced here**. Do not cite it
as if it were.

What *is* verifiable are the operative documentary rules, and they entail the answer.

**What counts as a passport.** INA 101(a)(30), 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(30), verified at
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title8/html/USCODE-2023-title8-chap12-subchapI-sec1101.htm>:

> The term "passport" means any travel document issued by competent authority showing the bearer's origin, identity, and nationality if any, which is valid for the admission of the bearer into a foreign country.

**Who is a competent authority.** 9 FAM 403.9-3(A)(1), verified at
<https://fam.state.gov/fam/09fam/09fam040309.html>:

> means an official who is duly authorized to issue passports by the government of the country of issuance.

**The closed US list.** 8 CFR 235.1(b), verified at
<https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-235/section-235.1>:

> A U.S. citizen must present a valid unexpired U.S. passport book upon entering the United States, unless he or she presents one of the following documents:

The enumerated alternatives are the passport card, Merchant Mariner Document, military ID,
NEXUS/FAST/SENTRI, closed-loop cruise documents, **Form I-872 American Indian Card**, a **tribal
document issued by a United States qualifying tribal entity** (the Enhanced Tribal Card scheme), and
children's documents. CBP's Western Hemisphere Travel Initiative page,
<https://www.cbp.gov/travel/us-citizens/western-hemisphere-travel-initiative>, gives the same list:

> U.S. citizens can present a valid: U.S. Passport; Passport Card; Enhanced Driver’s License; Trusted Traveler Program card (NEXUS, SENTRI or FAST); U.S. Military identification card when traveling on official orders; U.S. Merchant Mariner document when traveling in conjunction with official maritime business; or Form I-872 American Indian Card, or (when available) Enhanced Tribal Card.

(That is the land-and-sea list. The air list on the same page is shorter and carries the parenthetical
"note: the Indian/tribal card can NOT be used for air travel".)

Tribal **identity cards** are in. A tribal **passport** is not.

**Canada's rule.** IRPR (SOR/2002-227) ss. 50 and 52, verified at
<https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2002-227/FullText.html>:

> 50 (1) In addition to the permanent resident visa required of a foreign national who is a member of a class referred to in subsection 70(2), a foreign national seeking to become a permanent resident must hold (a) a passport, other than a diplomatic, official or similar passport, that was issued by the country of which the foreign national is a citizen or national;

> 52 (1) In addition to the other requirements of these Regulations, a foreign national seeking to become a temporary resident must hold one of the following documents that is valid for the period authorized for their stay: (a) a passport that was issued by the country of which the foreign national is a citizen or national, that does not prohibit travel to Canada and that the foreign national may use to enter the country of issue;

Both states define an acceptable travel document by reference to the issuing **country** of
citizenship or nationality. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is not an entity either state treats as
such a country, and the closed US-citizen document lists include tribal ID cards but no tribal
passport. The document is a genuine assertion of sovereignty and a real object; it is not a document
either border authority is empowered to accept.

## 6. Métis and Inuit

*Constitution Act, 1982*, s. 35(2), verified at
<https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/FullText.html>:

> (2) In this Act, aboriginal peoples of Canada includes the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada.

Section 35 recognises and affirms **rights** — aboriginal and treaty rights, extended by s. 35(3) to
rights under land claims agreements. It says nothing about nationality, status or citizenship. It is
a rights-recognition clause, not a nationality clause, and reading it as one is the single most
common error in this area.

Consequences:

- **Métis citizenship** — of the Métis Nation of Ontario, the Manitoba Métis Federation, and the
  other Métis governments — is citizenship *of a Métis government*. It appears nowhere in
  *Citizenship Act* s. 3, and Métis citizens are not "registered as an Indian under the *Indian
  Act*", so they do not attract even the IRPA s. 19(1) entry right in that capacity.
- **Inuit enrolment** under a land claims agreement (the Nunavut Agreement and its siblings) is a
  beneficiary designation under a s. 35(3) treaty. Not a route in *Citizenship Act* s. 3, and Inuit
  are not registered under the *Indian Act*, so s. 19(1) does not reach them qua Inuit either.
- On the US side Inuit and Aleut are expressly inside 8 U.S.C. § 1401(b) — "an Indian, Eskimo,
  Aleutian, or other aboriginal tribe" — but again only for a person **born in the United States**.

## 7. Why none of this becomes an atlas row

The atlas records routes to a **state nationality**: something that produces a passport recognised
under the Chicago Convention regime, consular protection, and a place in the visa-network graph the
map is built on. Tribal citizenship, Indian status, Métis citizenship and Inuit enrolment produce
none of those. Modelling them as citizenship routes would put nations into the graph that no other
state's visa policy addresses, and would imply to a reader that enrolling in a nation confers
travel rights it does not confer — which is precisely the misunderstanding the Haudenosaunee passport
episodes turn on.

The one thing here that *is* atlas-shaped is the § 1359 / 8 CFR 289.2 pathway, and it is a **US
permanent-residence route with an ancestry test**, sitting alongside the residence family rather than
the citizenship family. If it is ever added, it should be recorded as: United States, residence
status, origin test = at least 50 per centum of blood of the American Indian race and birth in
Canada, instrument 8 U.S.C. § 1359 with 8 CFR 289.2, basis as cited above. It confers permanent
residence, not citizenship, and no shortened naturalisation clock.

   or public policies for applicants with Canadian Indigenous ancestry is unsourced here.
2. **No official statement rejecting Haudenosaunee passports was obtained.** See § 5. The legal
   position is derived from the documentary rules, which is sound; the 2010 lacrosse episode is not
   sourced.
3. **The 1924 Act's Statutes at Large text is not on govinfo** (pre-1951 volumes absent). Cite it as
   codified, or via the 8 FAM 301.1-7 quotation above.
4. Two corrections to assumptions that circulate widely and were carried into the brief for this
   memo: 8 U.S.C. § 1359 reads "50 per centum", not "fifty per centum", and it contains **no**
   adoption-exclusion sentence — that language is in 8 CFR 289.1. And there is **no** Canadian Jay
   Treaty bill in LEGISinfo under any number.
